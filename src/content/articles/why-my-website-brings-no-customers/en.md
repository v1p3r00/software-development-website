Building a website does not automatically mean that the website will bring you customers.

It can have a modern design, impressive animations and a considerable development budget behind it. But if the right people cannot find it, cannot understand what you offer, do not trust the business, or simply do not know what to do next, the website is not doing its job from a business perspective.

**A good website is not simply one that looks good. It helps the right visitor take the right next step.**

Google's own documentation describes SEO as a way of helping people find your site through search, rather than simply a collection of tricks for search engines. ([Google Search Central – SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide))

Here are ten common problems worth checking before you decide that the answer is a completely new website.

---

## 1. Your customers cannot find you in search

Your website can be excellent and still generate very little business if the people who need your services cannot find it.

This is often an SEO problem.

It does not necessarily mean that your site has "no SEO". The problem can be much more specific:

- your pages do not clearly explain what you do,
- page titles are too generic,
- important services do not have dedicated pages,
- your content does not naturally reflect the language your customers use,
- Google cannot properly crawl or index parts of the site,
- internal linking is weak.

Google recommends using words that people actually search for in prominent and descriptive parts of a page, while keeping the content natural and useful. ([Google Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials))

### What should you do?

Do not start by stuffing keywords into every paragraph.

Instead, ask:

> What would someone type into Google if they needed exactly the service I provide?

Then check whether your relevant pages clearly address that search intent.

Google Search Console can help you understand which searches are already bringing people to your website. ([Google Search Central – Search Console](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/search-console-start))

**SEO is not about putting more keywords on a page. It is about making the right content discoverable for the right searches.**

---

## 2. Visitors cannot understand what you do quickly

A common problem with business websites is that the homepage tries to say too much without actually explaining anything clearly.

For example:

"Delivering innovative, professional and comprehensive digital solutions for modern businesses."

It sounds polished.

But a potential customer still does not know:

- what you actually do,
- who it is for,
- what problem you solve,
- or why they should keep reading.

Google's guidance also emphasises clear, well-organised and easy-to-follow content. ([Google Search Central – SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide))

### What should you do?

Answer three questions near the top of the homepage:

**What do you do? Who is it for? What problem do you help solve?**

For example:

"I build custom websites and web applications for small businesses that want a better online presence and fewer manual processes."

That is not particularly clever.

It is simply clear.

**Clarity is often more useful than clever copy.**

---

## 3. Your website is too slow

A slow website is not just a technical annoyance.

People have limited patience, particularly when they are browsing on a phone, using a slower connection or relying on an older device.

Google's Core Web Vitals measure three important aspects of user experience: loading performance, interactivity and visual stability. The current "good" targets are LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP at 200 milliseconds or less, and CLS at 0.1 or less, measured at the 75th percentile. ([web.dev – Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals))

That does not mean a website is good or bad based on one score alone.

Google explicitly says that page experience consists of multiple factors and that site owners should look at the overall experience rather than focusing on one or two signals. ([Google Search Central – Understanding Page Experience](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience))

### What should you do?

Start by checking:

- image sizes,
- server response time,
- third-party scripts,
- unnecessary animations,
- large JavaScript bundles,
- mobile performance.

PageSpeed Insights is a useful starting point.

Do not obsess over getting every number into the green.

**The real goal is to make the experience fast enough for real visitors to get what they came for.**

## 4. There is no clear next step

A visitor has read about your service.

What happens next?

This is where many business websites stop thinking.

There may be a "Contact" link somewhere in the navigation and a phone number in the footer, but the visitor is not given a clear path forward.

Not every visitor is ready for the same action.

Someone might want to:

- request a quote,
- ask a question,
- see previous work,
- check pricing,
- book an appointment,
- simply learn more.

### What should you do?

Give important pages a logical next step.

For example:

```text
Service page → Case study → Request a quote
```

Or:

```text
Blog article → Related service → Contact
```

Your CTA does not need to say "Click here".

Make it specific:

- "Request a quote"
- "Discuss your project"
- "Book a consultation"
- "View our work"

**If you do not make the next step clear, some visitors will simply leave rather than work it out themselves.**

## 5. Your website does not create enough trust

Visitors are not only asking what you sell.

They are also asking whether they can trust you with their money, time or project.

This matters particularly for professional services, where a customer often has to make a decision before they have experienced your work.

Research from Nielsen Norman Group has found that factors such as visual design quality, clear disclosure, comprehensive and current information, and connections to the wider web all contribute to how people assess a website's credibility. ([Nielsen Norman Group – Trustworthiness in Web Design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/))

### What should you do?

Do not simply write:

"We provide high-quality service."

Show evidence.

Depending on your business, that could include:

- customer testimonials,
- real case studies,
- previous projects,
- a genuine team introduction,
- company details,
- relevant experience,
- partner or client logos where you have permission,
- clear contact information.

"More than 10 years of experience" is a claim.

A specific project is evidence.

For example:

**"We redesigned the enquiry process for a 12-person professional services company, giving potential customers a clearer route from the website to an initial conversation."**

Specific information is generally more useful than broad promises.

## 6. Your content is too thin or too vague

"Modern solutions. Expert team. Tailored services."

These phrases may look good in a design mock-up, but they do not answer many customer questions.

A potential customer is usually wondering:

- What exactly do I get?
- Is this suitable for my business?
- How much might it cost?
- How long does it take?
- What happens after I enquire?
- What problem does it solve?
- What happens if I need support?
- Why should I choose you?

Google's current guidance emphasises helpful, reliable, people-first content that provides genuine value rather than content created primarily to manipulate search rankings. ([Google Search Central – Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content))

### What should you do?

Do not automatically add more words.

**Answer more real questions.**

A useful service page might include:

- a short explanation,
- the problem,
- the solution,
- the process,
- examples,
- expected outcomes,
- frequently asked questions,
- a clear next step.

The objective is not to make the page longer.

The objective is to leave the visitor with fewer unanswered questions.

## 7. The mobile version is worse than the desktop version

It is no longer enough to check your website on your own monitor.

Google considers mobile usability an important part of page experience, and Google uses a mobile crawler as its default crawler for websites. ([Google Search Central – Technical SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/get-started))

The problem is not necessarily that the site completely breaks on mobile.

It may simply be uncomfortable to use.

For example:

- text is too small,
- buttons are difficult to tap,
- horizontal scrolling appears,
- images make the page slow,
- menus are awkward,
- important information is pushed too far down,
- forms are difficult to complete.

### What should you do?

Test the site on more than one device.

Try:

- a smaller phone,
- a larger phone,
- a tablet,
- a slower connection.

And more importantly, use the website as if you were a first-time visitor.

**A mobile website is not simply a smaller version of the desktop website.**

## 8. You explain what you do, but not why it matters

Another common content problem is that the business talks extensively about itself without explaining the customer benefit.

"We have been operating for 15 years."

Fine.

But what does that mean for the customer?

"We use modern technologies."

Which customer problem does that solve?

"We are customer-focused."

How would a customer know?

### What should you do?

Translate business features into customer benefits.

For example:

**Feature:** "Online appointment booking."

**Customer benefit:** "Your customers can book appointments around the clock without having to send an email."

Or:

**Feature:** "Custom web development."

**Customer benefit:** "You do not have to reshape your business process around a generic software platform."

The difference may look small, but it changes the conversation.

**Do not only explain what you do. Explain why it matters to the person reading the page.**

## 9. You do not measure what happens on the website

This is one of the hardest problems to notice because nothing looks broken.

Without measurement, you are mostly guessing.

Perhaps your homepage gets plenty of visitors but very few reach the contact page.

Perhaps one service page generates most of your enquiries.

Or perhaps visitors regularly leave at a particular stage.

Google Analytics can measure user interactions as events, including actions such as link clicks and purchases. ([Google Analytics – Set up events](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/ga4/events))

### What should you do?

Do not only look at page views.

For a service business, you could measure a journey such as:

```text
Visitor → service page → CTA click → contact form → enquiry
```

Useful events might include:

- contact form submissions,
- phone number clicks,
- email clicks,
- quote requests,
- appointment bookings.

Google Analytics also allows important events to be treated as conversions for measurement and optimisation. ([Google Analytics – Conversion](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9356034))

**If you do not measure the important actions, it becomes much harder to know what to improve.**

## 10. The website goes live and is then forgotten

This is not a technical problem, but it is extremely common.

The website launches.

Everyone is happy.

And then nothing changes for two years.

Meanwhile, your services change, new projects are completed, old information becomes inaccurate, and the questions your customers ask may change as well.

Google recommends keeping content useful, reliable and up to date, and its SEO guidance specifically suggests reviewing older content when necessary. ([Google Search Central – SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide))

### What should you do?

You do not need to redesign your website every few months.

But you should periodically check:

- whether contact forms still work,
- whether pricing and service information is accurate,
- whether your case studies are current,
- whether important links still work,
- which searches are bringing visitors,
- which pages perform well,
- where visitors leave.

**A website is not just a one-off project. It is a business tool that should occasionally be reviewed and improved.**

---

## What should you check first?

If your website is not generating enough enquiries, you do not necessarily need a complete redesign.

Start with this checklist:

- [ ] Can your customers find you using the searches they actually make?
- [ ] Is it obvious what you offer within the first few seconds?
- [ ] Does the website load quickly on mobile?
- [ ] Does every important page have a clear next step?
- [ ] Do you show genuine evidence that people can trust you?
- [ ] Does your content answer real customer questions?
- [ ] Is every important feature easy to use on mobile?
- [ ] Is it clear why a customer should choose you?
- [ ] Are enquiries and other important actions being measured?
- [ ] Has the website been reviewed and updated recently?

If several answers are "no", you already have a useful starting point.

You may not need a new website.

A handful of well-chosen changes can sometimes be more valuable than rebuilding everything from scratch.

---

## More traffic is not necessarily the answer

It is easy to confuse website performance with visitor numbers.

But traffic alone is not a business result.

If 100 relevant people visit your website and none of them knows how to contact you, the problem may not be traffic.

The problem could be that the website is:

- difficult to find,
- difficult to understand,
- slow,
- unconvincing,
- awkward on mobile,
- or unclear about what the visitor should do next.

**Instead of only asking "How do I get more visitors?", ask "What happens to the visitors I already have?"**

That question often leads to a much more useful improvement plan.

---

## What is happening on your website right now?

If your website generates fewer enquiries than you expected, the first step is to understand where the journey breaks down. It might be an SEO issue, but it might also be that the right visitors are already arriving and simply are not given enough clarity, confidence or opportunity to get in touch.

Look at your website today as if you were a potential customer seeing it for the first time. **What do you understand within the first 30 seconds, and do you know exactly what to do next?**
