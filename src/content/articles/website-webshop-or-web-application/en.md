When a small business decides to improve its online presence, one of the first questions is often surprisingly difficult to answer:

Should you build a website, an online shop, or a web application?

From the outside, they can look quite similar. All three can run in a browser, use the same domain and share many of the same technologies.

From a business perspective, however, they solve different problems.

**The right choice is not the one with the most features. It is the one that solves the actual problem your business has.**

> Do you need to present information, sell products, or let people actually perform tasks online?

---

## Start with the basic difference

There is no perfectly strict technical boundary between these three categories. A modern website can contain application-like functionality, and an online shop can include quite sophisticated software.

For a business owner, however, it is useful to think about them in three broad categories.

### Website: when information is the main purpose

A traditional business website is primarily there to communicate information.

It might be:

- a company website,
- a consultant's website,
- a restaurant website,
- a portfolio,
- a professional services website,
- a manufacturer's online presence.

The visitor normally comes to learn something, compare services, view examples or get in touch.

```text
Google → website → learn about the service → contact the business
```

That does not mean a website has to be basic.

It can include multiple languages, a blog, animations, contact forms, booking systems, maps, integrations and a content management system.

The important distinction is that **information and communication remain at the centre of the experience.**

The web itself has some useful advantages here. Content can be accessed through URLs, shared and discovered through search engines, while the same web technologies can work across different devices and operating systems.

## Online shop: when the website needs to sell

An online shop goes one important step further.

It is not simply presenting products. It needs to support a transaction.

That usually means handling things such as:

- products,
- variants,
- prices,
- stock,
- baskets,
- customer details,
- delivery,
- payments,
- orders,
- confirmations,
- returns or related processes.

A typical flow might look like this:

```text
Product → basket → checkout → payment → order → confirmation
```

The checkout is particularly important because the customer is making a decision at this point.

A visually attractive shop does not automatically make a good shop. The buying process needs to be understandable, predictable and reasonably easy to complete.

Baymard Institute's current research puts the global average cart abandonment rate at around 70%. That figure is based on international research and should not be treated as a prediction for an individual Hungarian shop. It does, however, illustrate how much of the online shopping journey can end without a completed purchase.  

[Baymard Institute – Reasons for Cart Abandonment](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ecommerce-checkout-usability-report-and-benchmark)

Baymard's research also identifies checkout complexity and usability problems as important sources of friction.  

[Baymard Institute – Cart & Checkout Usability Research](https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability)

**An online shop is therefore not simply a product catalogue. It is an online sales process.**

---

## Web application: when the system needs to do work

A web application is different because its main purpose is usually not simply to present information or sell something.

It allows users to perform tasks.

For example:

- manage customers,
- book appointments,
- manage projects,
- allocate employees,
- create quotations,
- manage stock,
- generate reports,
- upload documents,
- manage subscriptions,
- use a customer portal,
- operate an internal business process.

Consider an appointment system:

```text
User logs in → selects service → chooses a time → system checks availability → booking is created → confirmation is sent
```

The browser is still involved, but this is no longer simply a collection of pages.

The system has to understand business rules, store information, manage users and often communicate with other systems.

That is the key difference.

**A web application is software delivered through the web.**

Modern web applications can also become progressively more app-like. Progressive Web Apps, for example, can use standard web technologies while offering capabilities such as installation, offline operation and background functionality where supported.  

[MDN – Progressive web apps](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps)

## So what is the practical difference?

A simple way of looking at it is this:

| Solution | Main purpose | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| Website | Information and lead generation | Company website |
| Online shop | Selling products online | E-commerce store |
| Web application | Processes, data and tasks | Customer or internal system |

The boundaries are not absolute.

A website can include an appointment system. A shop can have a customer portal. A web application can have a public marketing website.

**These are not three completely separate technologies. They are three useful ways of describing the main business purpose of a web project.**

## When is a website enough?

A website may be all you need if:

- you sell services rather than products,
- customers mainly need information,
- you want to generate enquiries,
- you need to present your company and team,
- you want to showcase previous work,
- you want people to find you through search,
- customers do not need complex accounts or workflows.

For example, a small accounting firm may get much more value from a fast, clear website explaining its services than from a custom-built client management platform.

**More software does not automatically create more business value.**

If the underlying business process is simple, the digital solution can often be simple as well.

---

## When does an online shop make sense?

An online shop becomes appropriate when selling online is an actual part of the business model.

It can make sense if you:

- sell physical products,
- have a catalogue of products,
- receive regular orders,
- want online payments,
- need automated order notifications,
- need delivery options,
- need to manage product variants or stock.

The important thing is to think beyond the product page.

Ask yourself:

> What happens after somebody clicks “Buy”?

For example:

```text
Order → payment → stock check → invoice → warehouse → delivery → customer notification
```

If half of that process is still being copied manually between spreadsheets and emails, the shop may only have digitised the first part of the problem.

Eurostat continues to track e-commerce activity across businesses and company sizes, with current datasets covering data through 2025. The statistics also distinguish between different enterprise sizes and industries, which is useful when considering digital sales as part of a broader business process.  

[Eurostat – E-commerce sales of enterprises by size class of enterprise](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/isoc_ec_esels/default/table)

## When should you consider a web application?

A web application becomes more interesting when your business has a process that is currently handled through:

- spreadsheets,
- emails,
- paper,
- several disconnected systems,
- repetitive manual administration.

Imagine a simple process:

```text
Customer enquiry → email → spreadsheet → employee processes it → another email → status update
```

A dedicated system could turn that into:

```text
Customer enquiry → system → task created → employee assigned → status updated → customer notified
```

The point is not to make the interface look more impressive.

The point is to reduce unnecessary manual work and make the process easier to manage.

**The strongest reason for building a web application is usually a business process, not a technology preference.**

## What does each option cost?

There is no universal price because the scope of two apparently similar projects can be completely different.

The following are **guide prices**, rather than official market averages.

### Business website

For a smaller, custom business website, a rough starting point might be around **€750–€2,000**, depending on the number of pages, design requirements, content, languages and integrations.

A more complex corporate website can obviously go considerably higher.

### Online shop

A smaller custom online shop might have a guide budget of around **€1,500–€4,000**.

The final cost depends heavily on product structure, payment and delivery integrations, stock management, languages, customer accounts and the amount of custom functionality required.

### Web application

Web applications have a much wider range.

A relatively simple internal tool might start around **€2,500–€7,500**, while a larger business platform with multiple user roles, integrations and complex business rules can cost substantially more.

**The cost is driven more by the complexity of the problem than by the number of pages.**

Ten simple pages can be easier to build than one complicated business workflow.

## Do you actually need custom development?

Not necessarily.

This is one of the most important questions to ask before starting a project.

If your requirements are fairly standard, an existing platform may be perfectly suitable.

For example, if you need:

- a basic company website,
- a standard online shop,
- a blog,
- a simple booking system,

there may already be a product that solves most of the problem.

That can reduce initial cost and shorten the time needed to launch.

Custom development becomes more attractive when your business process does not fit comfortably into an existing system.

For example, you might have unusual pricing rules, complicated approval processes, several types of users, specific integrations or a workflow that is unique to your company.

**Custom software is not automatically better. It is useful when the standard options stop fitting the business.**

---

## A simple way to decide

Before speaking to a developer, answer these five questions.

### 1. Do customers mainly need information?

If yes, start by considering a website.

### 2. Do customers need to buy products online?

If yes, you probably need an online shop.

### 3. Do users need to perform tasks inside the system?

If yes, look at web application functionality.

### 4. Are employees spending significant time on repetitive administration?

If yes, there may be an opportunity for a custom system.

### 5. Does an existing platform already solve the problem?

If yes, you may not need custom development at all.

This approach is often more useful than starting with questions about frameworks, programming languages or hosting.

---

## The right technology starts with the business problem

A small business does not automatically need a custom web application because it sounds more sophisticated.

Equally, a business selling physical products should not assume that a basic website is enough.

The right answer depends on what the online system actually needs to accomplish.

A local professional service may need nothing more than an excellent website and a straightforward enquiry process.

An online retailer may need a full e-commerce workflow.

A company with complex internal processes may benefit from a web application that replaces spreadsheets, emails and repetitive administration.

**The goal is not to build the biggest system. The goal is to build the smallest system that solves the right problem properly.**

---

## Which part of your business would you actually like to improve?

If you're unsure whether your next project should be a website, online shop or custom web application, start by describing the business process rather than the technology you think you need.

Write down what customers currently do, what your employees currently do, and where information gets copied or handled manually. That usually reveals much more than a list of desired features.
