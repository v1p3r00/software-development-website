## You do not need to automate everything

It is easy to talk about automation as if every manual task should eventually be replaced.

That is the wrong starting point.

Businesses contain plenty of work that still needs people. Someone needs to make decisions, speak to customers, negotiate, solve problems and deal with unusual situations.

Other tasks happen in almost exactly the same way every time.

An email arrives.

Someone fills in a form.

Data has to be copied into another system.

A confirmation needs to be sent.

A report needs to be prepared.

An invoice needs to be issued.

**These predictable, repetitive workflows are where automation can often create the most value.** Microsoft and IBM both identify frequent, repetitive, time-consuming and error-prone processes as strong candidates for automation. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

---

## Before you automate, understand the process

One of the most common mistakes is immediately looking for a tool.

“We need AI.”

“We need a Zapier workflow.”

“We need a chatbot.”

The better first question is:

> What actually happens today?

Take a quote request:

```text
Customer submits form
        ↓
Email arrives
        ↓
Someone reads it
        ↓
Data copied into Excel
        ↓
CRM record created
        ↓
Customer receives a reply
        ↓
Someone follows up later
```

You may not need to automate one individual task.

You may need to simplify the whole workflow.

Mapping the process first helps reveal unnecessary steps, delays, errors and manual data transfers. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

## What makes a good automation candidate?

A few simple criteria can help you decide.

### 1. How often does it happen?

If something happens several times a day or week, it is worth investigating.

If it happens twice a year, it probably should not be your first automation project.

### 2. How much time does it take?

A two-minute task is not necessarily interesting by itself.

But if it happens fifty times a day, the picture changes.

### 3. How rule-based is it?

The more predictable the process, the easier it usually is to automate.

For example:

> “When a new quote request arrives, send a confirmation and create a CRM record.”

That is a good automation candidate.

Compare it with:

> “Review the customer's problem and decide what we should offer them.”

That requires considerably more human judgement.

### 4. How often do mistakes happen?

Manual data entry creates opportunities for typos, incorrect values and missed steps. One potential benefit of automation is that clearly defined, rule-based tasks can be performed more consistently. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

### 5. What happens if it is not done on time?

This can matter more than the number of minutes involved.

If a missed follow-up causes you to lose a potential customer, the value of automation is not just the time saved.

## A simple automation score

You do not need a complicated business model.

Give each workflow a score from 1 to 5:

| Criteria | 1 point | 5 points |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Rare | Several times a day |
| Time required | A few minutes | Several hours |
| Error risk | Low | High |
| Rule-based | Many decisions | Almost always the same |
| Business impact | Low | Directly important |

Then look at the total.

**This is not a mathematical law. It is simply a prioritisation tool.**

Microsoft's automation guidance similarly recommends evaluating factors such as frequency, complexity, accuracy, effort and expected return when deciding which workflows to automate. :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

## 1. Email handling

Email is one of the obvious examples.

Not because every email should be answered by AI.

Because many emails are part of the same recurring process.

For example:

```text
New customer enquiry
        ↓
Identify the topic
        ↓
Find / create CRM record
        ↓
Create task
        ↓
Send automatic acknowledgement
```

In a simpler case, it could be as basic as:

> “Thanks for getting in touch. Someone from our team will contact you shortly.”

Automation does not necessarily replace the human response.

**It makes sure the enquiry does not disappear into someone's inbox.**

## 2. Quote requests

Quote requests often contain a surprising amount of manual work.

A customer submits a form, and someone then has to:

- read it,
- organise the information,
- enter it into the CRM,
- send it to the right colleague,
- acknowledge the enquiry,
- follow up later.

That can become an automated workflow:

```text
Quote request
    ↓
Validate information
    ↓
Create CRM record
    ↓
Assign salesperson
    ↓
Automatic confirmation
    ↓
Follow-up task
```

These workflows are strong candidates because they are triggered by an event, contain rule-based steps and often happen repeatedly. IBM lists email notifications, data handling and invoicing among common business automation examples. :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}

---

## 3. Invoicing

Invoicing is another area where the boundaries of automation matter.

Some parts of a simple, recurring invoicing process can be automated effectively.

For example:

```text
Service completed
        ↓
Create billing data
        ↓
Generate invoice
        ↓
Check
        ↓
Send
        ↓
Accounting system
```

Microsoft's documentation describes automated invoicing as something that can be fully automated, partially automated or kept manual depending on the complexity of the contracts and process. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}

In Hungary, invoicing processes also connect to the NAV Online Invoice system, so automation needs to take tax and compliance requirements into account as well. :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}

**The goal should not simply be to automate invoicing so nobody has to think about it. The process should remain controllable, traceable and compliant.**

---

## 4. Reports and weekly summaries

This is one of the most underestimated areas.

If someone spends every Monday morning:

- copying data from Excel,
- checking the CRM,
- taking numbers from the online shop,
- calculating totals,
- creating charts,
- sending an email,

there is probably an automation opportunity.

For example:

```text
CRM
 +
Online shop
 +
Invoicing
        ↓
Automatic data collection
        ↓
Report
        ↓
Weekly email
```

Modern workflow systems can not only automate tasks but also help measure process performance and identify bottlenecks. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}

**If you build the same report every week from the same five systems, it is a very strong automation candidate.**

---

## 5. Copying data between systems

This is classic low-value administrative work.

For example:

```text
Website
   ↓
Email
   ↓
Excel
   ↓
CRM
   ↓
Invoicing system
```

If the same information has to be manually copied into several places, you are not just losing time.

**Every additional copy is another opportunity for an error.**

If the systems provide APIs or suitable integrations, data can often move directly between them.

You do not always need custom software.

For simpler workflows, an automation platform may be enough.

These tools typically start with a trigger and then perform a predefined series of actions. :contentReference[oaicite:19]{index=19}

## How much is automation worth?

This is where ROI comes in.

You do not need a complicated financial model.

Imagine that an employee:

- spends 45 minutes a day on a repetitive administrative process,
- does it 20 working days a month.

That gives you:

**45 × 20 = 900 minutes**, or **15 hours per month**.

If you estimate the employer's hourly cost at €15, that is:

**15 × €15 = €225 per month**

This is only an illustrative calculation.

If automation costs €1,125 to implement and genuinely replaces €225 worth of work each month, the theoretical payback period would be:

**€1,125 / €225 = 5 months**

Real-world calculations are more complicated. You also need to consider maintenance, error handling, subscription costs and whether the freed-up time is actually used for higher-value work.

**The goal is not that every automation must pay back within five months. The goal is to establish a sensible relationship between the investment and the business value it could create.**

Microsoft recommends measuring outcomes such as time saved, error rates, cost per transaction and cycle time when assessing automation results. :contentReference[oaicite:20]{index=20}

## When should you not automate?

This is just as important as knowing what to automate.

### A task that happens rarely

If something happens twice a year, the automation may cost more to build than the time it saves.

### A constantly changing process

If the rules, exceptions and business logic change every week, maintaining the automation can become expensive.

### Complex human judgement

If the task requires experience, negotiation, empathy or serious judgement, it may not be suitable for full automation.

### A badly designed process

This is particularly important.

> **Do not automate a bad process simply because it can be automated.**

If a workflow contains three unnecessary steps, remove them first.

Then automate what remains.

Microsoft similarly recommends identifying redundant or unnecessary steps and bottlenecks before automating a process. :contentReference[oaicite:21]{index=21}

## Not every automation needs AI

This distinction matters.

If the task is:

> “When a new order arrives, send a confirmation.”

you probably do not need AI.

A simple rule-based workflow is perfectly adequate.

If the task is:

> “Read the customer's free-text enquiry, understand what they need and route it to the right process.”

then AI may make sense.

In simple terms:

```text
Rule-based process
→ traditional automation

Free-form language / complex interpretation
→ AI + automation
```

**AI is not a synonym for automation.**

Many business workflows can be automated more cheaply and reliably with straightforward rules.

## The best first automation is often boring

Your first automation project does not need to be an AI agent.

It might be:

- automatic quote-request processing,
- CRM record creation,
- acknowledgement emails,
- invoicing,
- weekly reports,
- overdue-task reminders,
- data transfer between two systems.

These are not necessarily exciting.

But if they run every day or every week, they can create continuous value.

**Automation succeeds not because the technology looks impressive, but because it removes unnecessary work from a process.**

## How should you get started?

Do not try to automate the entire business at once.

Write down the recurring tasks from the last week.

For example:

```text
Email replies
Quote requests
Data entry
Invoicing
Reporting
Follow-ups
Appointment scheduling
File organisation
```

Then mark:

- how often each happens,
- how long it takes,
- how many errors it causes,
- how rule-based it is,
- what happens when it is missed.

By the end, you will probably have one or two workflows that practically ask to be automated.

**That is where you should start.**

## Which workflow would you automate first?

Do not begin by choosing an AI tool or automation platform. First look at which workflow happens most often, consumes the most time, creates the most errors or causes delays for customers.

The best automation can be very simple. If it frees up several hours every week, reduces mistakes and leaves important human decisions with people, it can create significant business value.
