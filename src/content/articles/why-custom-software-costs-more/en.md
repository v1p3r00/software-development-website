## Why does custom software cost more upfront?

When a business needs a new system, two options usually appear quickly:

- subscribe to an existing application,
- build a custom one.

The first option can look dramatically cheaper.

A subscription of €50, €150 or €300 per month feels very different from a custom project costing tens of thousands of euros.

And in many cases, the off-the-shelf product really is the better choice.

**The problem starts when you compare only the initial price rather than the total cost and business value over the life of the system.**

Microsoft's Azure Well-Architected Framework recommends considering development resources, infrastructure, maintenance, support, licensing and subscription costs together when making a build-or-buy decision. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

---

## Off-the-shelf software is not necessarily cheaper

With a SaaS application, you are usually paying for an existing service.

The provider takes care of things such as:

- the application,
- infrastructure,
- updates,
- bug fixes,
- parts of the security workload,
- support.

That means you do not need to fund a large development project before you can start using it.

Instead, you keep paying over time.

For example, consider this simple illustration:

| Option | Initial cost | Monthly cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| Off-the-shelf application | €0–€750 | €75–€250 |
| Custom software | €12,500–€25,000 | €125–€500 |

**These are illustrative figures, not market price lists.**

The off-the-shelf application may clearly be cheaper during the first year.

But what happens after five years?

A €250 monthly subscription becomes:

```text
€250 × 12 × 5 = €15,000
```

And that may not include:

- additional users,
- premium features,
- API usage,
- extra modules,
- custom integrations,
- migration,
- implementation work.

This is why total cost of ownership, or TCO, matters. TCO looks at the full lifecycle cost of a product or service, including direct and indirect costs rather than just the initial purchase price. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

## Why is custom software expensive?

Because somebody has to build it.

With an off-the-shelf product, the development cost is spread across many customers.

If 5,000 businesses use the same SaaS product, the provider can develop one application and recover that investment across its customer base.

A custom system is different.

It is built around your processes.

That means someone needs to create:

- the frontend,
- the backend,
- the database,
- authentication,
- permissions,
- administration,
- APIs,
- integrations,
- error handling,
- testing,
- deployment.

**The higher upfront price is partly the cost of creating something that did not exist before.**

---

## Development time is only part of the equation

Building software is not simply a matter of someone “coding a few pages”.

Before development starts, somebody needs to understand:

- the business process,
- the rules,
- the data,
- the users,
- the permissions,
- the integrations.

Then comes design and implementation.

After that:

- testing,
- bug fixing,
- deployment,
- monitoring,
- documentation,
- maintenance.

This is why software cost should not be estimated purely by looking at the number of developer hours.

The whole lifecycle matters.

---

## Your business logic has a price

One of the biggest advantages of an off-the-shelf product is that it already solves a known problem.

If you buy a ready-made invoicing system, for example, you are not paying to develop invoicing from scratch.

Custom software is different because it can implement exactly how your business operates.

That might include:

- a unique approval process,
- custom pricing,
- specialist customer management,
- unusual workflows,
- custom reports,
- a specific permissions model.

The further your business processes are from what an existing product expects, the more custom development may be required.

> **The real question is not “Do we need a CRM?” but “Do we need a CRM that works in a way existing products cannot reasonably support?”**

---

## Integrations can change the economics

Imagine that you have found an off-the-shelf application that seems to do almost everything you need.

But it also needs to connect to:

- your webshop,
- your invoicing system,
- your CRM,
- your stock system,
- your internal application.

Now the cost is no longer just the subscription.

You may also need:

- API fees,
- integration services,
- custom development,
- middleware or iPaaS,
- data synchronisation,
- ongoing maintenance.

Microsoft's build-or-buy guidance highlights exactly this trade-off: buying can offer faster deployment and lower upfront costs, while customisation and long-term maintenance need to be considered separately. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

---

## What happens when the off-the-shelf product changes?

This is an often overlooked risk.

You do not control the product roadmap.

The provider may decide to:

- introduce new features,
- change an API,
- increase prices,
- restructure pricing plans,
- remove a feature,
- retire an older version.

That is part of the SaaS model.

Subscriptions can be billed monthly or annually, while the provider continues to operate and maintain the product. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

This is convenient.

But it also creates vendor dependency.

**With off-the-shelf software, you generally adapt to the provider's product strategy. With custom software, you control the product strategy.**

---

## Custom software is not “pay once and forget”

This is another common misconception.

A custom system still has ongoing costs.

You may need:

- hosting,
- database infrastructure,
- backups,
- monitoring,
- security updates,
- dependency updates,
- bug fixes,
- new features,
- third-party API maintenance.

Microsoft's guidance also points out that custom solutions require significant upfront investment as well as ongoing maintenance. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

With SaaS, much of this work is handled by the provider.

That responsibility transfer is one of the things you are paying for with the subscription.

---

## Ownership matters

With a custom application, one important question is:

> “Who actually owns the software?”

A development contract does not automatically answer every aspect of this.

You should clarify:

- who owns the source code,
- what licence you receive,
- where the application is hosted,
- who controls the database,
- who has server access,
- how another developer could take over,
- which third-party components are used.

This becomes particularly important for software that is central to your business.

**One of the biggest advantages of custom software is control.**

Not necessarily because you need to manage every technical detail yourself, but because the system can be built around your business rather than around someone else's product roadmap.

---

## When is off-the-shelf software the better choice?

Quite often.

If the problem is common and several mature products solve it well, there is usually little value in rebuilding it.

Examples include:

- project management,
- video conferencing,
- general accounting,
- email marketing,
- basic CRM,
- document management,
- appointment scheduling.

If an existing product:

- handles your workflow,
- has acceptable pricing,
- provides the integrations you need,
- meets your security and data protection requirements,
- and does not create unacceptable vendor dependency,

then custom development may simply be unnecessary.

Microsoft's application platform guidance similarly recommends custom development primarily for unique, high-value areas, while using off-the-shelf solutions elsewhere. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

---

## When does custom software make sense?

Custom development becomes more interesting when software itself is an important part of how the business operates.

For example:

- your workflows are genuinely unusual,
- existing products require too many compromises,
- several systems need to work as one process,
- custom automation matters,
- the software creates a competitive advantage,
- large amounts of manual work can be removed,
- control over your data and processes is critical.

At that point, you are not simply buying a piece of software.

**You are building part of your business infrastructure.**

## TCO: compare the whole picture

A useful comparison looks more like this.

### Off-the-shelf application

```text
Subscription
+ implementation
+ user licences
+ extra modules
+ integrations
+ data import
+ migration
+ support
+ switching costs
= total cost
```

### Custom software

```text
Planning
+ development
+ testing
+ deployment
+ hosting
+ maintenance
+ security updates
+ further development
= total cost
```

TCO exists precisely to bring one-off and ongoing costs, as well as direct and indirect costs, into the same calculation. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

---

## A simple five-year example

Imagine an off-the-shelf application costing:

- €375 per month,
- €1,250 implementation,
- €2,500 for integrations and initial work.

Over five years:

```text
€375 × 60
+ €1,250
+ €2,500
= €26,250
```

Now imagine a custom system costing:

- development: €20,000,
- initial deployment: €1,250,
- hosting and basic operations: €200 per month,
- maintenance and smaller improvements: €175 per month.

Over five years:

```text
€20,000
+ €1,250
+ (€200 × 60)
+ (€175 × 60)
= €43,250
```

**In this example, the custom system is still more expensive after five years.**

And that is perfectly fine.

Perhaps it automates processes that would otherwise require several hours of manual work every week.

Perhaps it gives the business functionality that no existing product offers.

TCO is not only about spending less.

You also need to consider what the software produces in return.

---

## Not every cost appears on the invoice

Imagine that an off-the-shelf system requires an employee to spend three hours every week copying information between two systems.

Another solution automates the process.

The subscription may be cheaper.

But the business is still paying for the manual work.

It is worth considering:

```text
annual manual labour
+ cost of errors
+ duplicate data entry
+ lost time
+ integration costs
+ subscriptions
```

**The cheapest software is not necessarily the solution with the lowest total cost.**

---

## Custom software is not automatically the right choice either

Custom software has clear advantages:

- flexibility,
- control,
- custom business logic,
- control over the roadmap,
- custom integrations.

But it also has disadvantages:

- higher upfront cost,
- longer implementation,
- maintenance responsibility,
- dependence on developers,
- ongoing technical decisions.

Off-the-shelf software offers:

- faster deployment,
- lower initial cost,
- proven functionality,
- vendor support,
- ongoing updates.

Its disadvantages can include:

- recurring subscription fees,
- limited customisation,
- vendor dependency,
- integration limitations,
- having to adapt to the provider's roadmap.

---

## So which should you choose?

Start with one question:

> “Is my problem genuinely unique, or is it a common business problem?”

If it is a common problem, start by looking at existing products.

If your business operates in a genuinely unusual way and the software needs to reflect that, custom development becomes more interesting.

There is also a third option: **combine the two.**

For example:

- off-the-shelf CRM,
- off-the-shelf invoicing,
- off-the-shelf email platform,
- custom business application,
- custom integrations.

You do not need to build everything yourself.

The goal is to build only the parts that genuinely differentiate your business or create significant operational value.

### The question is not simply which one is cheaper

The better question is **which solution produces the required business outcome at an acceptable total cost over its useful life.**

An off-the-shelf application is often the sensible choice.

Custom software becomes more compelling when flexibility, automation, control or unique business logic are worth more than the lower initial cost of a ready-made product.
